A PERSONAL DOCUMENTARY / NINE CHAPTERS

My story,
still becoming.

It didn’t begin with a career plan. It began with a box of parts, a sensor I didn’t recognise, and the urge to find out what it did.

“What can I make
with these parts?”

An electronics kit / around sixth standard

CHAPTER 01 / THE BEGINNING

Before I knew the names, I wanted to know how.

My father bought me an electronics kit when I was around eleven or twelve, in sixth standard. Motors and circuits caught my attention before I had the words for much of it. I liked being able to make a thing move, then work out which part had made it happen.

School gave that curiosity somewhere to go. I made STEM exhibition projects, including a hydraulic bridge and a Ferris wheel. I kept bringing ideas to the exhibitions, partly because I liked making them and partly because I liked standing there and explaining them.

CHAPTER 02 / LEARNING BY EXPERIMENTING

The sensor I mistook for a disco light.

I wanted to make a robot vacuum, so I took a remote-controlled car and adapted it with downward-facing motors to act as sweepers. I didn’t know much about programming yet; the first version was more about trying a physical idea than building a finished autonomous cleaner.

Then I ordered a sensor because I thought it looked like a disco light. It turned out to be a PIR sensor. I didn’t know how to use it, so I went to YouTube, found Arduino, and started testing sensors one by one. Later I ordered a set of roughly 27 different sensors, most with names I had never heard before.

A short Arduino course gave me a little more structure. I was still learning by taking things apart, searching, and trying small experiments rather than following a straight curriculum.

Mistake the part.
Find the whole new field.

Curiosity does not always take the shortest route

CHAPTER 03 / THE AI RABBIT HOLE

A new tool, then ten new questions.

My interests shifted toward generative AI around the end of seventh standard. I started with tools such as ChatGPT, tried prompt writing, and followed the possibilities into AI filmmaking and image generation.

At a free AI course, one passing mention stayed with me: you could run models locally. I went looking for what that meant, tried Ollama and ComfyUI on my laptop, and learned that what a computer can do depends a lot on the hardware underneath it. I couldn’t run everything I wanted, but learning why was part of the appeal.

I also explored automation with Make and n8n, and connected tools through MCP. One early AI-assisted experiment gave me analysis I couldn’t trust. It was a useful reminder: connecting tools can make a workflow look impressive without making its answers correct. I still use AI-assisted coding while I build my programming skills; it helps me explore an idea, but I want to understand what the code is doing.

CHAPTER 04 / BEYOND THE CLASSROOM

One exhibition, many moving parts.

For my school’s 50th-anniversary exhibition, I contributed to several subject displays. The Computer Science room became the main focus: I helped prepare Meta Quest VR experiences, made different virtual worlds and animations, recorded VR clips, and put together presentations and demonstrations.

I also contributed to other displays, including a hydraulic model for the STEM area. The exhibition meant long days of setup and a lot of coordinating things that had to be ready at the same time. I spent most of the event in the Computer Science room, so I barely got to see the other work I had helped prepare.

What I remember most is the scale of the effort: a project is not just the clever part. There is setup, explaining, troubleshooting, and looking after all the details people might otherwise never notice.

There isn’t a photograph from the anniversary exhibition in the supplied archive, so this chapter uses the story rather than a mismatched event image.

Build the experience.
Then help it happen.

CHAPTER 05 / UNDERSTANDING COMPUTERS

I wanted to know what was under the case.

In seventh standard I got my first laptop. It became a doorway into another kind of hardware curiosity: CPUs, graphics cards, memory, motherboards, storage, and why some machines can run a model that others cannot.

I started comparing PC configurations and looking at single-board computers such as Raspberry Pi. That led me to servers and network-attached storage as ideas to understand, not equipment I had already built. I found myself asking how a computer changes when you stop thinking of it as one device and start looking at the parts and jobs inside it.

Video editing became another way to use the computer: I made school event teasers and enjoyed the rhythm of putting visuals together, while knowing I was still learning the craft.

CHAPTER 06 / FROM DEVICES TO SYSTEMS

The fun was in seeing how the pieces connect.

Once I learned about home servers, I kept following the connections outward: NAS, networking, Wi-Fi access points, virtual machines, and systems such as TrueNAS and Proxmox. I also explored smart-home protocols including Zigbee, Matter, Thread, Wi-Fi, and Z-Wave, and tried Home Assistant on an older computer.

These interests often begin as diagrams or plans rather than installed systems. I have spent time designing hypothetical home networks and smart-home setups, and more recently exploring ROS 2 and Gazebo simulations for robotics. I’m still figuring out which parts I want to build physically and which questions I need to answer first.

The shift was from asking “What device should I get?” to asking “How would the whole thing work together?” That systems view is something I want to keep developing.

A device is a start.
A system is a story.

Networks / automation / robotics

CHAPTER 07 / BUILDING AND COMPETING

An idea has to leave the screen and face questions.

At a school AI Thon in Class 9, I brought ClassEcho, an idea for capturing classroom lessons and making summaries and personalised revision material. I was still learning to code, and the idea was not selected. It gave me a reason to think about who a tool is for and what problem it should solve.

At the Middle East College Sustainability Hackathon, my teammate and I presented an early transparent solar-window concept called AURS. We later developed the presentation as AETHER for the Indian School Muscat Techathon. We did not place at those events. The judges’ feedback that a functional prototype would strengthen the idea gave me a clearer next question.

AETHER has changed as I researched it. I moved from a broad idea about agricultural-waste pigments toward exploring luminescent materials, carbon quantum dots, and edge-mounted photovoltaic cells. These are research directions and proposed design elements—not a validated material recipe or measured energy output.

At SIC Conclave, AETHER received third place. Later, at the EurekaDev Hackathon, it received third place in the Research track. AETHER was also featured by Muscat Daily. Those moments mattered, but they do not settle the science; the next step is still to test the idea and find out what actually works.

CHAPTER 08 / THE ENGINEER I’M WORKING TOWARDS BECOMING

Hardware and software. Both sides of the question.

For a while, my answer to “What do you want to be?” changed all the time. In 2026 I started putting those interests together and looking toward Electronics and Communication Engineering. I’m drawn to embedded systems because they sit between the physical parts and the software that makes them useful.

Semiconductors, RF engineering, robotics, SLAM, PCB design, and EV power electronics are areas I want to understand more deeply. I’m still a student; these are directions I’m curious about, not qualifications I already have.

I want to get better at the fundamentals, make more complete prototypes, and understand the whole system around a component—not just collect a list of technologies to say I know.

Not just what it does.
Why it works.

An ambition / not a credential

Follow the question.

CHAPTER 09 / WHAT I’M STILL LEARNING

Curiosity is a start. It needs direction.

I can get pulled a long way into a new subject. One small question can lead to the next tool, then the system around it, then a different field entirely. That’s how I have found some of the most interesting things I’ve learned.

It can also make it hard to decide what to finish first. I’m still learning how to give an idea enough time to become a working experiment, and how to balance exploration with the things I need to study. I don’t want to hide that behind a story that makes every attempt look like a win.

I use AI tools, but I want to understand what they give me. I like new hardware, but I want to know what it does. I have plans, but I’m learning to separate a plan from something I have built. That’s the point I’m at: curious, learning, and trying to make the next step more deliberate.
